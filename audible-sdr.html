Audible SDR

By Ali Raheem, G3ALI · g3ali.com · Source on GitHub

A software-defined radio, moved down into the audio band so you can hear every stage. The “RF” is a 32 kHz-sampled signal with six transmissions between 3 and 15 kHz buried in noise, including G3ALI calling CQ in Morse. Each stage of an I/Q receiver is applied in order, and you can look at and listen to the signal after each one. The I/Q buttons play I in the left ear and Q in the right.

On the air

Each Play button scales its own buffer to a comfortable level, so quiet stages are still audible. To compare levels, use the spectra: they share one dBFS scale, where a full-scale sine reads 0 dB.

1

Antenna

analog

Everything arrives at once. Listen and you can pick out the AM carrier as a steady 3 kHz whistle, two Morse signals at 11 kHz (a slow beacon and G3ALI calling CQ), a narrowband FM tune at 12.75 kHz and the 15 kHz data signal, which many adults barely hear. The two programmes on the 7.5 kHz ISB station are there too, but at this point they sound like scrambled noise. Drag on the spectrum to tune the receiver.

2

RF preselector

analog

A band-pass filter before the mixer. An ideal multiplier would not need it, but real mixers also respond at harmonics of the LO (3×, 5×…) and can be overloaded by strong signals far from the wanted one. The preselector stops the mixer from ever seeing them. The grey trace is the antenna signal and the green curve is the filter. Drag on the spectrum to move it.

3

Local oscillator

analog

One oscillator, two outputs 90° apart: LOI = cos(2πfLOt) and LOQ = −sin(2πfLOt). Played separately they sound identical. The difference is only phase, which the X–Y plot shows as a circle; with no quadrature it would collapse to a line. The frequency is rounded to a multiple of 0.244 Hz so the loop stays seamless. Square gives a switching-mixer LO with odd harmonics, band-limited below Nyquist.

4

Mixers

analog

Each path multiplies the preselected signal by its LO, which makes sum and difference frequencies. A station at fLO + Δ lands at Δ and at 2fLO + Δ. The I output on its own cannot tell fLO + Δ from fLO − Δ: both fold onto the same audio frequency. With the ISB preset, play I alone and you hear the melody and the bass line on top of each other. Q has the same spectrum magnitude but a different phase, and that phase is what separates them later.

Real mixers are not perfect: the Q branch may have a little more gain than I, the LO split may not be exactly 90°, and LO leakage adds DC. Section 8 lets you add those faults here and then remove them digitally.

5

IF filter

analog

The same low-pass filter on I and on Q. In a zero-IF receiver the “IF” is baseband, so this is the channel filter and the ADC’s anti-alias filter in one. It removes the sum products near 2fLO and anything outside the channel. It is also the anti-alias filter for the ADC in the next section: switch it off there and see what folds in. Drag on the spectrum to set the cut-off.

6

ADC and sample rate

analog→ digital

The IF output is still a continuous voltage. A pair of ADCs samples I and Q at a rate fs, and from then on the receiver only knows the band −fs/2 … +fs/2. Anything outside it does not vanish. It folds back into the band, which is called aliasing, and once it has folded no later filter can separate it from the wanted signal. Lower the sample rate and the spectrum narrows to the green band. With the IF filter in circuit nothing harmful folds in. Switch it off and stations from all over the 16 kHz “RF” band land on top of the one you wanted.

Because I and Q are sampled together, the usable band is the full ±fs/2, twice what a single real ADC at the same rate covers. Real SDRs usually sample fast (an RTL-SDR runs at a few MS/s) and then lower the rate digitally in stages with CIC and half-band filters. Each stage follows the same rule as here: filter first, then drop samples. Lower rates make every later step cheaper.

7

Complex baseband, I + jQ

digital

The samples are now numbers in a computer. Treat each pair as one complex sample z = I + jQ. Its spectrum is no longer mirror-symmetric: whatever was above the LO is now at positive frequencies and whatever was below it is at negative frequencies. The X–Y plot traces z in the complex plane.

8

IQ mismatch and correction

part 2analog faultdigital fix

Everything so far assumed a perfect quadrature mixer. Real ones have two I and Q paths built from separate parts, so they never match exactly. With a gain ratio g and a phase error φ the Q path delivers g·sin(θ − φ) instead of sin θ, and the LO leaking into the mixer adds a DC offset to each output. Look back at the LO’s X–Y plot in section 3 while you move the sliders: the circle becomes a tilted ellipse.

The damage shows up as an image. A signal at +Δ also appears, weaker, at −Δ, and the other way round. How much weaker is the image rejection ratio. A 1 dB gain error alone limits it to about 25 dB, and 1° of phase error alone to about 41 dB. That is enough to hear the lower sideband of the ISB station leak into the upper one, which is exactly the job the I + jQ receiver was meant to do.

1 · Inject the fault

These errors are applied in the analog part of the receiver: gain and phase to the Q output of the LO in section 3, and DC offsets at the mixer outputs in section 4. The image is easiest to see with the CQ preset, where two clean tones appear on the positive side, and easiest to hear with the ISB upper preset.

2 · Estimate it from the signal and correct it

The receiver cannot see the fault directly, but it can measure statistics of the received signal. The means give the DC offsets. The ratio of standard deviations gives the gain error. The correlation between I and Q gives the phase error, because ρ = sin ψ with ψ = −φ. The correction is then Q′ = (Q/g − I·sin ψ) / cos ψ. This assumes the true I and Q are uncorrelated and of equal power. That holds for most signals but not for a lone carrier at exactly 0 Hz, so try the AM preset with a fault and watch the estimate go wrong.

9

DFT, mask and shift

digital

Take the DFT of the whole loop of z (131 072 points), multiply it by a mask that keeps only the part you want, then roll the bins to move that part along the frequency axis. Because positive and negative frequencies are now separate, the mask can pick a single sideband. The image that stage 4 could not separate is now simply somewhere else on the axis. Drag on the top spectrum to place the mask. The shift moves every component by the same number of hertz, which changes the pitch you hear, and harmonics stop being harmonic, which is why mistuned SSB sounds like Donald Duck.

The receiver switch shows what all of this buys you. A single-mixer receiver only has I, a real signal whose spectrum is always mirror-symmetric. Its mask can only be mirrored too, and it cannot shift: moving a real signal’s positive half up moves its negative half down. A real direct-conversion receiver tunes with its LO instead. Try it with the ISB presets: the melody and the bass line come out together whatever you do with the mask.

10

Inverse DFT and detector

digital

The inverse DFT gives a complex time signal again, but a speaker needs a real one. Turning z into audio is the detector’s job, and each mode of transmission wants a different one.

Product detector, Re{z}
For SSB, CW and FSK. A classic receiver multiplies by a beat-frequency oscillator (BFO) and keeps the real part. Here the shift in section 9 is the BFO.
Envelope, |z|
The diode detector of a crystal set. It ignores phase, so tuning errors do not matter, but it distorts when one sideband is missing or the carrier fades.
Synchronous AM, Re{z·e−jφ̂}
A phase-locked loop follows the carrier and rotates it onto the real axis: a product detector that tunes itself. It still works on a single sideband plus carrier, which halves the noise bandwidth and lets you avoid interference on the other side. Try the “Synchronous AM” preset, then switch to the product detector.
FM discriminator, arg(z[n]·z*[n−1])
The phase step between samples is the instantaneous frequency. This only works with I and Q, because a real signal has no phase to measure. On the FSK station it recovers the data bits as a square wave.

Filters are applied in the frequency domain over the whole 4.096 s loop, so they are zero-phase and the loop repeats without a click. The preselector and IF filters use Butterworth magnitude responses of the chosen order. Mixing happens at the 32 kHz simulation rate, so sum products above 16 kHz alias back down; the IF filter removes them just as it removes the in-band ones. Spectra are Welch averages of 32 Hann-windowed 2048-point DFTs.
